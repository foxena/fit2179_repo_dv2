What it takes to run beyond marathon distance

A 50 km run is easy to treat as a single standout effort. This story asks a more useful question: what does the build-up look like, and how does one recreational runner’s 50 km effort compare with Australian ultra-marathon results?

Privacy note: the personal training export has been reduced to activity summaries only. Raw GPS routes are not included, so home locations and regular starting points are not exposed.

The question: when does a run become something bigger?

The personal training log contains 99 runs of at least 20 minutes. Most runs were ordinary training sessions, but one reached 50.15 km. The first view sets the scale before the story moves into public Australian running data.

Running is popular, but much of it happens outside formal events

AusPlay provides the public participation backdrop. It shows why event results can never describe the whole running population: many people run independently, while organised races and clubs capture only part of the story.

The required map uses ABS state/territory boundaries as a stroke-only outline and AusPlay state/territory activity-frequency values as bubbles. It provides a broad Australian activity context without exposing personal GPS data.

A ranked bar chart places running/jogging among common adult activities, showing why running is familiar even to non-race participants.

The age profile shows who is most likely to participate in running/jogging, which helps explain why running feels more visible in some life stages than others.

Comparing any participation with organised participation shows how much running sits outside formal events.

When running is organised, the organising body varies, reinforcing that there is no single “running system” capturing all activity.

The public benchmark: Australian ultra-running results

To compare the 50 km effort with actual running data, the Kaggle ultra-marathon dataset was trimmed to Australian ultra events from 2015–2022 and aggregated before publication. The result is a lightweight comparison layer with no athlete names, IDs or individual race records.

Public ultra-marathon results show that 50 km is the most common of the selected Australian ultra categories, while 100 km and 100 mile events remain smaller specialist fields.

The bubble plot combines finishers, median finish time and median speed to show how distance changes both scale and difficulty.

The highlighted band shows where the 50.15 km training run sits within grouped Australian 50 km speed results.

Finish-time bands translate pace into a more intuitive benchmark for a general audience.

Age and gender patterns show why a single “average 50 km runner” is too simple.

The build-up: consistency matters before the big distance

The training log makes the public story concrete. Volume arrives in waves, training days cluster around routine, and pace changes with distance and elevation. The 50 km run is the endpoint of a pattern, not just an isolated outlier.

Layering distance and activity count shows whether higher monthly volume came from more outings or longer outings.

Weekly totals make consistency and interruptions easier to see than individual activity dots.

The calendar heatmap turns routine into a visual pattern of active and quiet days.

Distance, pace and elevation are combined to show why one “average pace” can be misleading.

Heart rate adds physiological context to the terrain story, showing that harder routes are not only about distance.

A custom view: effort, not just distance

Distance alone rewards long days but misses hard short efforts. The effort matrix uses derived data: a relative score based on average heart rate, speed and elevation gain per kilometre.

This custom-built visualisation combines derived scoring with a bubble matrix rather than relying on a single standard chart.
